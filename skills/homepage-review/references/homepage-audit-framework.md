# The Narrative Pivot's 3-Question Homepage Audit Framework

Source: The Narrative Pivot, Issue No. 3, "the accidental homepage audit: what B2B SaaS
gets wrong every time (and the 3 questions to fix it)."

This is Neera's own framework — not April Dunford's, not any other published positioning
methodology. It exists because most B2B homepages fail to answer three simple questions
within the first 10 seconds of a visit, and that gap is what's costing them booked demos.

The worked examples below (with their original headline/subheadline rewrites) are quoted
from the source essay for calibration purposes — they show how a grade was reasoned
through, including how a rewrite might look. The skill itself does not generate a
rewrite when run; that step is intentionally left to the reader.

## The 3 Questions

1. **What exactly is this product or service?** Are they a category disruptor, a
   consolidation of multiple tools, or an alternative to the status quo? (And,
   underneath that positioning angle: what is the product, literally?)
2. **Who is it for?** The target buyer should see themselves in the hero section. Not
   buried halfway down the page.
3. **Why should your target audience care?** The transformation moment — the
   before/after shift your product creates for the buyer. Not just features or benefits.
   The changed state from their current struggle to the outcome they want.

Most homepages answer none of these in the hero. Most answers, when they exist at all,
show up halfway down the page.

## Worked Examples (calibration set)

### hex.tech — Grade B

**Headline:** "The AI analytics platform for your whole team." Near-identical to
positioning used across the BI category (see Amplitude).

**Subheadline:** "Finally — anyone in your business can get trusted data insights from
advanced analytics to simple questions in one integrated platform." Generic — swap "Hex"
for "Tableau" or "Looker" and the sentence still works. No competitor differentiation.

**Applying the framework:**
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is it? | A data hub where everyone from data analysts to marketers can upload data to analyze, question, and act on it relative to their goals. |
| Who's it for? | Hero says "your whole team." Rest of the homepage implies anyone from Sales to CS to Data Science, though integration examples/testimonials skew toward engineers and data analysts. |
| Why should a customer care? | Buried in the FAQ: Hex replaces multiple data notebooks, BI tools, and one-off requests with a centralized platform, so teams can answer their own questions without filing Slack requests. |

**What works:** Eventually states the status quo ("juggling separate notebooks, BI
tools, and ad-hoc Slack requests") and positions itself as a unified replacement.

**What can be better:** All 3 answers exist, but they're buried under generic language
and AI buzzwords. The consolidation story — the actual value — requires scrolling to
find.

**The rewrite:**
- Headline: "Your data team shouldn't be a factory for ad-hoc requests."
- Subheadline: "Unify fragmented data across notebooks, BI tools, and Slack in one
  workspace. Run the analysis once, let everyone from engineering to marketing now
  self-serve effortlessly."

This names the specific audience (data team), names the exact tools being replaced
(notebooks, BI tools), and states the transformation (self-serve effortlessly) — instead
of just claiming "AI-powered."

### statsig.com — Grade C

**Headline:** "Measure what ships. Ship what matters." Short, memorable.

**Subheadline:** "Test how each release — from features to AI-powered experiences —
lands with customers, so you can protect trust and optimize at the speed of AI."
Undermines the headline with buzzword-heavy copy ("optimize at the speed of AI" doesn't
mean anything concrete). Fails to name the primary buyer or the pain point Statsig
solves.

**Applying the framework:**
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is it? | A multi-tool platform for building products more systematically. |
| Who's it for? | After reading the full homepage: primarily product managers, but also usable by engineering, dev ops, and data science. No single named buyer in the hero. |
| Why should a customer care? | Features are listed but never connected to a pain point. No transformative moment vs. status quo or competitors anywhere on the page. |

**What works:** Prospects can install the SDK and run their first experiment directly —
letting buyers test the product independently is a real differentiator.

**What can be better:** No competitive frame of reference, no transformation moment,
buzzword-heavy subheadline. Strong ingredients, weak assembly into a clear,
buyer-specific message.

**The rewrite:**
- Headline: kept as-is (memorable).
- Subheadline: "Product owners should be able to scale experiments without overloading
  engineering. Feature flags, A/B tests, and analytics, all in one platform. Decouple
  code from releases and ship with confidence."

Picks ONE primary buyer (product owners — the people who feel the pain of slow
experiment cycles) instead of vaguely serving PMs, engineers, and data science teams at
once, which resonates with no one specifically.

### semgrep.dev — Grade A

**Headline:** "Code Security for Builders" — semi-identifies the primary buyer; the
subheadline clarifies further (developer/engineering background, catching/flagging
vulnerabilities, unifying SAST/SCA/secrets). Validated by the logos section: "Loved by
leading engineering teams."

**Has a dedicated "Why Semgrep" section** — this is the differentiator vs. Hex and
Statsig. It states the transformation directly: giving security teams "visibility,
control, and confidence." A transformation point doesn't need a whole section or grand
language — it just needs to succinctly state what the product does differently (replace
something? beat the status quo? offer something competitors don't?).

**Applying the framework:**
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What is it? | A security platform that scans source code for vulnerabilities and enforces guardrails/coding standards — custom rules or AI-powered detection. |
| Who's it for? | Developers, CISOs, and AppSec teams — explicitly labeled under a "Built For Every Role" section. |
| Why should a customer care? | Goes beyond shrinking the backlog (pain) and increasing engineering velocity (business impact) — it "learns as you code," building memories of past decisions to prevent future false positives. |

**What works:** Solves the "too many ICPs at once" problem other homepages stumble on —
via a clearly labeled "Built for every role" section that states what value *each*
specific ICP gets. This is the one acceptable way to address multiple ICPs; note the
homepage still leads with ONE primary buyer signal in the hero ("builders") first.

**What can be better:** Very technical throughout; a line like "Empower invention
without friction" reads as corporate jargon even for a technical audience that has no
patience for it. Better sourced from actual customer language ("Find real
vulnerabilities, skip the false alarms").

**The rewrite:**
- Subheadline: "Find real vulnerabilities, ignore the noise, fix before shipping. Unify
  SAST and SCA into one platform and see 80%+ fewer false positives with significantly
  faster triage and remediation."

Keeps the category-signaling language (SAST, SCA) so the target buyer recognizes
themselves, while cutting the jargon that doesn't parse even for a technical reader.

## Takeaways

The difference between A/B/C comes down to whether the homepage answers the 3 questions
up front, or makes the visitor hunt for the answers:
- **Hex buried its differentiation** (exists, but takes scrolling to find) → B
- **Statsig skipped it entirely** (no transformation moment anywhere) → C
- **Semgrep led with it** (clear hero signal + dedicated section + segmented ICPs) → A

When drafting rewrites, always: name the specific audience, name the specific pain/tools
being replaced, state the transformation — and keep it tight (no unexplained jargon).
